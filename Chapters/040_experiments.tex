\chapter{Experiments}

In this chapter we want to test our proposed architectures on several datasets, presented in \ref{eval_datasets}. 
First, we will pretrain our models on a large 2D dataset, using different sampling and augmentation strategies. After that, we target specific datasets to finetune our models on. With these weights, we compare inference performance on various datasets. Finally, we will conduct some experiments with the 3D feature network.

The qualitative evaluation of the dataset's variability is based on the correlation analysis of Cellpose, between the style vectors (global average pooling of CNN Cellpose feature maps at bottleneck) of dataset subsets. Visual inspection is done for those datasets that have not been in the correlation study of Cellpose. Note that a more sophisticated approach would be to pursue a similar approach to the method used in the original Mediar paper, where they analyse the clustered t-SNE representation of the encoder embeddings. However, this approach would also require to manually pick the amount of k clusters for the k-means algorithm, if no elbow heuristics are present for a within-cluster sum of square plot. We chose a similar weighting of the sample probabilities to the CellposeSAM paper, but scaled down the weighting of the Cellpose dataset, as they weighted it up to 59\%, which would be almost 60 times higher than the weights for most other datasets they used. Note that the probability of an image of a certain dataset being batched also scales with the dataset size.

For the Mediar network with the MiT SegFormer encoder module, we use the pretrained ImageNet-1k weights from the SegFormer paper. The networks with the Hiera encoder are initialized with the SAM2 image encoder registry, pretrained on the SA-V dataset. We discard the multi-phase training approach from the original Mediar training pipeline, because it was tailored for the unsupervised NeurIPS challenge dataset. In short, what they do in their multi-phase training is training two separate models that have different levels of bias towards the target modalities. This ensembling only has meaningful impact if the target datasets have a lot of different modalities such as it was the case in the NeurIPS challenge dataset. So for training a model that generalizes well over unseen modalities and that is able to be finetuned for one target modality, training just one model is considered the best choice. To achieve that single generalizable model, we follow the same path as CellposeSAM by pretraining the model on basically every public 2D dataset there is. The resulting dataset described in Section \ref{section_pretraining_datasets} is almost four times larger than the pretraining data from Mediar. For the networks with the Hiera encoder, we use the SAM2 image encoder registry, pretrained on the SA-V dataset.
All models are trained for 80 epochs with a batch size of 64, distributed over four H100 GPUs with model weight wrapping Multi-GPU training. The optimizer is set to AdamW with an initial learning rate of 5e-5, decaying using cosine scheduler with intervals of 100 without restarts. For the lightweight decoder we use PyTorch autocast, amplifying the training by autocasting images to float16. Due to numerical stability restrictions, this can not be applied to the MA-Net decoder based architectures, slowing down training. Due to accumulating memory in the default Mediar backbone, a custom image loading function is used to enable pretraining on nodes with limited RAM. Batched images are preprocessed and augmented runtime in the dataloader, which slows down training to a certain extent, but is far superior in providing diverse training images to prevent overfitting. Because of runtime augmentation, we do not precompute ground truth flows due to the spatial and intensity transformation invariance of the flow generation transform. \subsection{Pre-Evaluation of Pretraining Results}

Before declaring the pretraining step as finished and official runs for finetuning and inference benchmarking can be scheduled, the models have to be tested briefly. In general, the way we perform official pretraining is very expensive, as we schedule runs with multiple GPUs, as single GPU training limits batch size to 7 for consumer grade GPUs (\textless24GB VRAM). As for the CLAIX High-Performance-Computing cluster of RWTH Aachen, a single GPU hour is billed as 24 CPU-core hours. Consequently, using 4 GPUs is billed for 96 core-hours, resulting in just 6 possible runs on the cluster until the monthly user quota is reached. Consequently, tuning sampling ratios and other training parameters is hard. As we expect a similar sampling ratio-performance dependence for all models, it is advisable to test different configurations on one model. The final configuration was selected after 8 runs. It is expected, that performance can be tuned to a higher standard, if systematically scheduled tuning is feasible for the available computational resources. As mentioned above, due to limited resources, this was not the case for our environment. 

We evaluate the candidate pretrained models, by performing zero-shot inference on the CTC data and the BlastoSPIM dataset. It turned out, that the lightweight configuration of the SAM2 extension for Mediar performed slightly worse on exclusively all tests, compared to the Mediar-SAM configuration with the MA-Net Decoder. As depicted in Table~\ref{tab:4.3}, the majority of the weights are in the encoder. This suggests, that there is not much computational time saved and performance does not vary much too. We concluded that the negligible time saved at inference does not make up for the performance loss, so we decided against scheduling any more tuning and benchmarking for this model. The other networks performed very similarly, so we will focus on comparing the performance of the default Mediar weights (referred to as \textit{paper weights} or \textit{from\_phase1}), the Mediar network with the SAM2 encoder module (referred to as \textit{MediarSAM}) and the Mediar network with extended pretraining.   

\section{Model Finetuning}
\label{Finetuning}

In this section, we take the pretrained models and finetune them on specific target datasets, as presented in Section \ref{eval_datasets}. In general, the aim of finetuning is to optimize the model weights themselves and the image processing pipeline around the model to maximize accuracy for inference on specific datasets. So when working in a very target oriented environment, it is often common to not only train the model weights on the specific datasets, but also edit the pipeline around the model. For example, if there is knowledge about physical constraints concerning spatial cell properties or reflection coefficients, this dataset specific knowledge can be directly incorporated into the pre- and postprocessing. For instance, known reflection coefficients can be referred to a certain range in pixel intensities after image acquisition. A subsequent histogram operation based on known intensities can be a powerful tool, to get rid of background cells. Spatial cell properties can be directly formulated as constraints in feature space. If there is a known min/max diameter, area or any numerical boundary for aspect ratios, extents, solidities or eccentricities, one could already eliminate most false positives. All of these tweaks can change the inductive bias to a large scale, making the model lose its ability to generalize well over different modalities. Since we aim to create generalizable models, we try to use as little resources as necessary for finetuning. We want to show, that our models only need a few ROIs for training, in order to reach high accuracy in inference. 

\subsection{Finetuning on Fully Annotated Data}

First, we want to finetune the models on fully annotated data. For this, we only use a few 2D slices of one training image. Although there is next to no anisotropy in the synthetic data, isotropic image acquisition is not always given for other datasets. We therefore train the models not only on axial slices, but on on YZ and XZ slices as well. For fully annotated data, the loss function does not have to be modified in such a way, that only annotated regions contribute to losses. So on a high level, the pretraining pipeline can be reused. We slightly decrease the learning rate to 2e-5 as suggested in \cite{lee2022mediarharmonydatacentricmodelcentric}. For model checkpointing, we will evaluate the models not on a certain number of epochs, but after a certain threshold for ROIs seen in training. We therefore implemented a checkpointing system, that saves the model weights at different order of magnitudes of ROIs (10, 100, 1000, 10000, 100000). This enables a more accurate comparison for model benchmarking, as epoch iterations depend on the number of training images. We sample from 80 manually picked slices, including all slicing directions Z, X and Y. We use a batch size of 4 for all pretrainings and apply the same runtime sample augmentations as in pretraining, as listed in Table \ref{tab:4.4}.

\subsection{Finetuning on Partially Annotated Data}

For partially annotated data, we mainly focus on the CTC datasets introduced in \ref{cha:3.2}. The limited annotations come along with a few challenges. First and most importantly, we have to adapt the way we optimize our network weights. At loss calculation, unannotated instances will produce unwanted gradients as false positives will dominate in dense cell regions with low annotation rates. We therefore use a masked loss, so only annotated regions will contribute to the cellprobability BCE and MSE flow loss. To prevent the network from predicting arbitrarily large shapes, as this would score the minimal loss for the BCE head, we dilate the loss mask by a few iterations to include 6-7 pixels around the annotations. As this might once again include unannotated instances, the dilation kernel size/iteration count can be adjusted based on the cell density. Another approach would be, to introduce some sort of Euclidean distance weighting, to reduce influence of predictions in unannotated regions far from annotations. As there was no significant difference between dilated masks and distance weighted loss, we used the dilated loss mask for faster runtime.

We also want to investigate, if cropping batched images to annotated regions improves learning progression. Conceptually, the regions outside the dilation range of annotations should not interfere with training gradients, as loss is only calculated close to annotated cells. We hypothesize, that cropping to annotated regions can lead to faster convergence, but cropping too much from the image will interfere with the forward pass, as the lack of context will limit the feature space to the statistics around annotations. To leverage the potential gap in statistics from training to test time, we include hard negative samples by manually cropping out patches of exclusively background pixels.
To test the masked loss training performance, we manually remove 80\% of annotations from a fully annotated training image of BlastoSPIM. We train the default Mediar network, which was extended to 3D inference and initialized with the pretrained weights of the paper before training it with the masked loss. Different cropping strategies are applied after batching training images. The first configuration crops the batched images to a small area around a randomly selected, annotated cell instance. This will be referred to as \textit{instance cropping}. Another configuration is, to revert random zoom and crop processing of the default pipeline and instead crop to the outer bounding box, including all labeled instances as well as 50 pixels of buffer. This will be referred to as \textit{ROI cropping}. Also, we trained a run with no cropping at all, giving the model maximum context for forward prediction. Lastly, we evaluate the impact of incorporating a small number of additional hard negative samples into the training set. The runs were trained for 50 epochs with a batch size of 4 and evaluated on a small test set of 10 images.

As shown in Table \ref{tab:cropping_results_vline} and Figure \ref{fig:cropping_results}, giving the model maximum context for forward prediction will score the highest instance segmentation accuracy. However, a run will also need significantly more resources, as the image size will be a lot larger as well. For large training images as it is the case for the CTC data, we conclude that for our purpose, which is to compare different architectural designs, the use of ROI cropping is justified, regarding the negligible performance loss and the significant computational resources saved. Including hard negative samples in masked loss training also seems to help reduce the statistic gap in training and test images. Notice that from an IoU perspective, the zero-shot model seems to perform best. This indicates that even with hard negative samples, the model is biased from the statistics around cells to such a degree, that the overall semantic segmentation between foreground and background is worse, while the instance mask contours improved significantly as indicated by the SEG scores. We reproduced this behaviour across several different datasets. Yet we chose BlastoSPIM as most representative for an official benchmarking for different masked loss training strategies, because the dataset is proven to be suitable for successful training under default conditions. We have shown that the masked loss function performs significantly better than using default loss. Hard negative samples seem to keep the training more stable. For our finetuning setup we conclude, that training with limited annotations can improve instance segmentation SEG scores, given that labeled instances sufficiently present the statistics of the test set. As the models get increasingly more biased towards the few labeled instances, the overall IoU seems to go down, as background regions are not represented in training and the model only gets feedback for positively labeled regions. Note, that for the way we extend Mediar to 3D inference, it is also important to learn features on the other planes too, in case of high anisotropy. To finetune the network for maximum performance, it might even be best to train separate models for each directional predictions. So for strong anisotropy, weights for different statistics could be saved in separate model paths, enabling the model to reload the state dict for each permutation in the prediction loop. Either way, this is just a suggestion for potential challenge submissions. For our purposes, we decided not to manually annotate further sagittal or coronal slices, because we mostly want to keep handcrafted tweaking for higher network performance away.

\section{Model Inference}
\label{Inference}

In this section, we test our pretrained models on several benchmarking datasets. Note that we do not aim for tweaking best performance on each model, but rather aim to compare generality and finetuning capability, measured at different datasets. Since we do not aim for a challenge submission within this thesis, we disabled checkpointing for highest IoU/loss. To compare models on datasets which are sufficiently labeled for training, we checkpoint at a certain number of ROIs seen in training for a fair comparison. This way, we can show how well the models adapt to different statistics in testing sets. To get a meaningful result, we perform a large grid prediction, iterating over datasets, models, training volume and inference parameters.

\subsection{Post-Processing Parameters}

As grid predictions get expensive really fast, depending on how many parameters to iterate over, we want to focus on the one with the most impact. Therefore, we use the Cellpose default parameters for the max pooling kernel size for maxima detection of cell candidate histograms and keep $11\times11\times11$ neighbourhood size for seed masks. For the cellprobability threshold, we want to investigate the prediction accuracy dependencies. Cellpose set this threshold to 0 per default, without explanation. StarDist's object probability learning objective acts very similarly in post-processing compared to the cellprobability threshold and impacts performance a lot. As empirically shown by initial tests, the cellprobability threshold also influences performance in Cellpose post-processing to a large degree. To find a generalizable threshold for each model, we include a small array for different thresholds in our grid inference, ranging over several order of magnitudes.

\subsection{Testing on synthetic Data}

The synthetic CTC dataset Fluo-N3DH-SIM has very similar statistics over different sequences and timestamps. As there is a significant amount of noise in the images, we expect the finetuned models to perform a lot better than the zero-shot model, as they adapt to the background statistics very fast. Also, label diameters extend over multiple objects, that one could assume to be similar instances and thereby creating over-segmentation. We choose 11 test images and iterate over all models with certain numbers of ROIs seen in training to investigate the performance progression over training. We compare performance for cellprobability thresholds ranging from 1e-6 to 0.9.


\subsection{Testing on BlastoSPIM}

As BlastoSPIM is also fully annotated, we expect the models to learn the statistics of the dataset fast. Although we only use 1 stack for training, we assume the network generalizes well due to high correlation between training and test sets. We again choose 11 fully annotated stacks as test images and iterate over several cellprobability thresholds and ROIs seen in training. 


\subsection{Testing on CTC Data}

This dataset is by far the most challenging. Annotations are limited to single instances within slices, also only for some timestamps and restricted to z-slices. For challenge submissions, it may be reasonable to manually tweak some heuristics of the images, as well as manually annotating XZ and YZ slices,
as cells appear differently in size and shape for non-axial slice directions. As mentioned above, for demonstrating generalizability and benchmarking models against each other in the same setting, these heuristics are not important. 
The main issue with this dataset is that actual 3D performance benchmarking may not be as representative as for fully annotated data, as evaluation will be summarized by individual 2D annotations on z slices. Example annotations are displayed in Figure \ref{fig:DRO_TRIC_Annotations}. Although inference is done in 3D for the test set, only a small fraction of the predicted stack will contribute to the evaluation. As there are plenty of instances labeled and thereby many images are used for testing, the small fraction of each image being evaluated might be compensated. Nevertheless, the actual 3D contours may not be measured sufficiently for a representative benchmark.

\subsubsection{Memory handling and runtime considerations}
Due to very large image sizes, the flow aggregation for multi-axis inference had to be rewritten to prevent memory accumulation. Also, the post-processing has been adapted to handle larger stacks. Alternatively, a shifted window processing could have also been implemented for post-processing, as it is already the case for network forward prediction. However, boundary artifacts are hard to compensate, even with overlapping windows. 
We integrated the memory friendly 3D inference code into the Cellpose branch as well. Even though the measures for improved memory handling speeds up inference, we noticed that runtimes for large 3D stack inference in the Cellpose environment are significantly higher, making predictions on test datasets with a substantial number of large-scale images too expensive. This is due to the fact, that the SAM-1 encoder from CellposeSAM is basically a vanilla ViT, which does not scale well with increasing image size (see Chapter \ref{cha:ViT}). As they also changed global attention on every layer, prediction times are usually significantly higher than all other models we benchmarked. This issue is investigated in a separate study (see \ref{runtime_benchmarking_section}). As we rely on every testing image in the CTC data, because there are limited annotations to evaluate on, we decided to exclude the CellposeSAM network from our studies on the partially annotated CTC data. We also decided to only quantitatively evaluate performance on the DRO and TRIC images, as TRIF makes even the faster models infeasible for large dataset-wide predictions (each image is ~3.3GB). Note that one could manually extract subvolumes for existent annotations or come up with a script that processes regex descriptors from label names, paired with an areal search for annotations to crop each image-label pairs to volumes around annotations. This could be handy for tuning on that specific dataset and maximize performance. However, we figured the DRO and TRIC sequences are sufficient for benchmarking partially annotated learning and inference performance. As depicted in Figure \ref{fig:CTCData}, DRO is comparable with TRIF in cell density and shapes. We therefore perform grid inference on the whole 02 sequence of DRO and TRIC, iterating over various models and probability thresholds for DRO and TRIC. For DRO, we only compare training progression at zero-shot and 1000 ROIs, as inference is quite expensive for the large-scale images. 


\subsection{Testing on Zebrafish Data}
\label{Testing_Zebra}

The Zebrafish data mainly consists of unlabeled data. However, there is one fully labeled stack, which we use for benchmarking zero-shot performance. Although the ground truth for this dataset consists of 3D labeled instances, the annotations are done slice wise. This is usually not problematic, if annotations are consistent across slices. Usually, one would generate machine based silver truth annotations, which can be refined by manual labeling using tools like QuPath for example. Note that this depends on the capabilities of the baseline machine generation tool, the dataset and annotator preference. In any case, slice consistency should be ensured by visual inspection across non-axial views or rendered vertices in a 3D engine. As displayed in Figure \ref{fig:ZebrafishAnnotations3d}, there is a significant amount of label noise due to inconsistencies across z-slices. We therefore use binary closing to fix intermediate empty annotations, if neighboring slices are annotated with the same integer ID. 
Zero-shot performance in this dataset suffers for generalizable models because of background cells with similar shapes, causing a significant amount of false positives. To fix that, one could perform few-shot training including background hard negatives. As background cells have similar statistics compared to foreground cells, we noticed a small degradation in performance for few-shot training. While metrics incorporating False Positives improved, the SEG score actually declined. Since the use-case tracking pipeline enables post-processing with detection masks, we used these masks instead, filtering out all False Positives from the zero-shot prediction. Using this pipeline, we performed grid inference over all models and several cellprobability thresholds. This way, we could also use the whole test image without having to discard some slices for few-shot training. Note, that testing and training on the same stack also does not provide a reliable evaluation of the generalizing capabilities of the testing models.

\section{3D Feature Network}

In this section, we outline the experiments conducted with the 3D Feature Network. Apart from numerous unit tests and sanity checks, little tuning was performed with this network. We have built a functional 3D runtime augmentation and training pipeline, but after some performance evaluations we decided to dedicate our resources into building and tuning other networks, as generalizability and accuracy were comparable, but eventually lower than our other methods. 

\subsection{Pretraining}

After ensuring a fully functioning training and dataloading pipeline, we prepared some fully annotated 3D datasets for pretraining. Now in general, there are two approaches for this network. First, one could gather as much labeled pretraining data as possible. Eschweiler et al. \cite{eschweiler2022robust3dcellsegmentation} showed the generalization capabilities of Conv3D decoders for robust 3D segmentation. As the inductive bias from the convolution operation makes the CNN decoder less data-hungry, we expect the network to be capable of generalizing with a few hundred pretraining images, given there is enough variability in the training data. 
Another approach is to briefly pretrain the weights with small, but diverse pretraining dataset. Subsequent finetuning to a target dataset would result in a specialist model for a specific dataset. 

Most publicly available 3D datasets with sufficient annotations originate from datasets like PlantSeg and thereby mostly cover membrane channels. As the data acquisition for 3D pretraining datasets is also highly time consuming, we decided to opt for the latter approach. We briefly pre-trained our decoder with the 01 sequence of the Synthetic images from the CTC dataset and also included some BlastoSPIM low-contrast training images. We train the network for 200 epochs and checkpoint with a small validation split, consisting of a subset of the training set. We use IoU-based metric checkpointing instead of validating best weights with losses. The encoder is initialized with the CellposeSAM checkpoint from the paper. The decoder weights are trained from scratch using He normal init. We linearly increase the learning rate from 5e-7 to 5e-5 and use a cosine scheduler afterwards with AdamW optimizer. As the decrease of training and validation loss was stable at all times, we used a batch size of 4, to keep computational resources in bounds. Training time took 52 hours, with a pretraining dataset of 90 3D images.

\subsection{Testing}

As mentioned above, we noticed reasonable results for the CTC Fluo-N3DH-SIM 02 sequence testset (0.70 mIoU, 0.54 SEG). Nevertheless, we did not gather enough pretraining data for generalizable prediction power. Zero-shot performance was significantly worse for any other cell modality than CTC SIM. Since few-shot performance seems to not perform better, we decided not to further work to tune network parameters and continued developing other methods.
